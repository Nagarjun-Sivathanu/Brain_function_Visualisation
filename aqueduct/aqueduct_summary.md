# Region Summary: Aqueduct (Cerebral Aqueduct of Sylvius)

**Region name:** aqueduct
**Hierarchy level:** 3
**Parent:** midbrain (mesencephalon)
**Synonyms:** cerebral aqueduct, aqueduct of Sylvius, mesencephalic aqueduct, iter

---

## 1. Overview

The (cerebral) aqueduct is a narrow, fluid-filled channel — roughly 1–2 mm in diameter — running through the midline of the dorsal midbrain (mesencephalon). It connects the **third ventricle** (above) to the **fourth ventricle** (below), making it the essential conduit for cerebrospinal-fluid (CSF) flow between the upper and lower ventricular system. It is surrounded by the **periaqueductal (central) grey matter**; anterior to it lies the tegmentum, and posterior to it lies the tectum (superior and inferior colliculi). Its walls are lined by ciliated ependymal cells.

## 2. Detailed Functional Description

- **CSF conduction.** The aqueduct is the sole channel through which CSF produced in the lateral and third ventricles passes to reach the fourth ventricle and then the subarachnoid space. It is critical for maintaining unobstructed CSF circulation.
- **Ciliary-driven flow.** Ependymal cells lining the aqueduct bear apical cilia oriented anteroposteriorly; their coordinated, metachronal beating helps propel CSF through the narrow channel.
- **Intracranial-pressure maintenance.** By keeping CSF moving from the upper to the lower ventricles, the aqueduct helps maintain stable intracranial pressure and protective buoyancy/cushioning for the brain.
- **Homeostatic transport.** As part of the CSF pathway it supports distribution of nutrients/signalling molecules and removal of metabolic waste.
- **Anatomical/clinical landmark.** Because it is the narrowest point of the ventricular system, the aqueduct is the most common site of obstruction; its surrounding periaqueductal grey is a key pain-modulation and defensive-behaviour centre.

## 3. Behavioural Description

The aqueduct has no behaviour of its own; its functional role is **keeping the CSF pathway open and flowing**. When it is patent, CSF circulates normally, intracranial pressure stays balanced, and the brain remains cushioned and chemically stable. When it is narrowed or obstructed (e.g., aqueductal stenosis, tumour, congenital atresia), CSF backs up into the third and lateral ventricles, producing **obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus** with raised intracranial pressure — headache, vomiting, visual disturbance, gait/cognitive changes, and, in infants, enlarging head circumference.

## 4. Functional Mimicry Notes (for agent modelling)

Model the aqueduct as a **narrow CSF-conduit service / pressure-critical chokepoint**: it accepts CSF inflow from the third ventricle and delivers it to the fourth ventricle, uses ciliary beating to maintain flow, and raises a high-priority alarm if flow is impeded (since obstruction rapidly threatens the whole system via hydrocephalus). It is infrastructure, not a decision-maker, and is embedded in the midline ("part of midbrain") substrate.
